Jaspers, Arendt, Rothko and the Boundary After Which One Cannot Return

There are times when a person is permitted to live in half-tones. Permitted to believe that complexity in itself is already a form of honesty. But there come epochs when that same complexity begins to serve not truth but evasion. Then the word can no longer be merely the accompaniment of thought. It must distinguish.

I

The Epoch When Nuance Becomes Evasion

There are times when a person is permitted to live in half-tones. Permitted to believe that complexity in itself is already a form of honesty. Permitted to guard the nuance, to refrain from conclusions, to hold contradiction as a sign of maturity and even of inner culture. But there come epochs when that same complexity begins to serve not truth but evasion. Then lack of clarity ceases to be attentiveness and becomes a way of not looking directly. Nuance no longer deepens — it conceals. And in such times the word can no longer be merely the accompaniment of thought. It must distinguish. It must not soothe too early. It must not adorn the fracture. It must not help the soul settle into the grey zone where it can repeat indefinitely that everything is intertwined and therefore nothing can be named completely.

✦
II

The Approach to Jaspers — Through Arendt

It is in precisely this state that I came to Karl Jaspers. Not from academic interest. Not from love of philosophy as tradition. Not from any wish to add another great name to my inner circle of interlocutors. I came to him through Hannah Arendt, trying to understand not simply Nazism as history, but the very possibility of historical cruelty. Not only the mechanism of totalitarianism, not only the structure of power, not only the technique of propaganda and terror — but that inner point at which a person ceases to be an obstacle to evil. Where exactly resistance disappears in them. At what moment language, memory, fear, career instinct, exhaustion and the refusal to think compose an environment in which the terrible no longer seems impossible.

✦
III

What Arendt Shows — and Where Analysis Ends

Arendt shows the mechanics of disintegration. She shows how the space between people is destroyed, how the common world ceases to be a place of encounter and becomes a system of functions, how evil can operate without demonic depth, almost without a face, almost without inner passion — through the sheer banality of the refusal to judge. In this lies her terrible strength. She strips the person of the comforting schema in which evil must always come from monsters. No — it can be superficial, bureaucratic, administrative, almost grey. It can operate through emptiness.

But at a certain point it became clear to me that analysis alone is not enough. To understand the mechanism is not yet to understand the position of a person inside it. One can see the structure of the fall but not know what to do with one's own place in that fall. And then Jaspers appears.

IV

Jaspers: Clarity as the Rarest Form of Endurance

I need him not as a historical authority and not as a museum figure of postwar German conscience. I need him as a person who found himself inside a collapse and did not allow the collapse to become the law of his thought. This is a rare constitution of personality. He lived in Germany, was married to a Jewish woman, was expelled from university life, existed in an atmosphere of threat and a shrinking world in which thought itself was ceasing to be innocent. And through all of this he preserved not a heroic posture, not beautiful inflexibility, not theatrical moral elevation, but something rarer and harder: clarity. The capacity not to allow catastrophe to enter language as a new norm. Not to let destruction become a habit of thought.

✦
V

Independence and Freedom Are Not the Same Thing

Here one distinction matters. Independence is not yet freedom. Independence can be temperament, stubbornness, the inheritance of character, a family or class bearing. Freedom in Jaspers is of a different order. It is the capacity to remain in the space of the common world without surrendering one's inner judgment to the crowd, the state, fear or the epoch. Not to step out of history, but to endure its pressure without allowing it to rewrite conscience itself. This is precisely why he matters now. Not as a philosopher of old Europe, but as a measure. As a person with whose help one can ask oneself not only what happened to the world, but what is happening to me when the world changes in such a way that the old innocence becomes impossible.

✦
VI

Humanitas: Not an Ideal but a Last Thread

And here in Jaspers appears another word — quiet but decisive: Humanitas. Not humanism in the scholastic or rhetorical sense. Not a general love for the human being. Not a consoling set of values. Humanitas in his work appears precisely there where the old culture has already failed to save, where education, intellect, tradition and high civilisation did not keep a person from falling. After this, Humanitas ceases to be a beautiful ideal and becomes almost the last inner thread.

It is what keeps a person from becoming a function. What prevents them from surrendering their judgment to the external world. What does not allow them to see in the other person only a role, a mass, a collective guilt, a useful symbol. Humanitas in Jaspers is not a gift and not a comfortable condition, but the effort not to destroy oneself from within when destruction has already become the environment. And therefore for me this word matters no less than his thought on guilt: without Humanitas, all distinction would become a cold intellectual gesture. With it, distinction remains human — even when it is merciless.

✦
VII

I Did Not Grow Up in Russia

I did not grow up in Russia. This must be said at once, clearly and without hesitation. I grew up in the Soviet Union, in Latvia, in an ordinary family, in a Latvian-Russian cultural environment tilted more toward the Russian side. For me, Russian space was not external. It was not an object of observation. It was the inner fabric of the world. I thought in that language, felt in it, grew up inside it. But precisely in the course of growing up, the difference began to emerge more and more strongly. Not at first as a political position, but as experience. In intonation. In the relationship to truth. In how force is spoken of. In where the boundary of the permissible lies. In how the state is perceived, other people's pain, one's own history.

I witnessed the transformation of Soviet space into Russian space not as an observer from outside, but as a person who lived within that environment. What changed was not only the state. What changed was the way of being in the world.

✦
VIII

The Transformation: How the Air Changes Before a Storm

Latvia and Russia were gradually ceasing to be simply two parts of a common past. They were diverging more deeply than could be described in political words — in their relation to freedom, to truth, to historical memory, to the right of the other to separateness. And this difference was not instantaneous. It accumulated, the way air changes before a storm. At first one notices only a different intonation. Then a different reaction. Then a different relation to borders, to force, to pain. Then one understands that this is already a different climate.

I need to say this because my text cannot be read as the text of a person who one day discovered evil in Russia and simply set himself against it. Everything is far more complex and therefore requires greater honesty. I lived too long in a space where this connection seemed natural, to now allow myself the ease of a definitive rupture without inner work. But it is precisely for this reason that the incision here must be deeper.

✦
IX

War Changes Everything: The Ontological Question

I am not Russian. I did not choose Putin. This is not my state. My country has long been different — Latvia. Then there was Dublin, and this was simply the movement of life, not a gesture of flight from future catastrophe. But the question did not disappear. If everything were resolved by a passport, the answer would be too simple. But language, memory, the inner geography of childhood, the family line, all that cultural fabric — none of it disappears along with legal status. It continues to live in the person. Therefore I cannot honestly say that what is happening does not concern me. But I also cannot say: this is mine. Between these two impossibilities arises that inner incision without which it is impossible to write this text at all.

War did not bring simply new information. Much was visible earlier. But it removed the very possibility of the previous position. The question changed. It became not only political but ontological. Where am I located when I continue to speak this language. In what sense am I bound to what is happening. Where is the line after which belonging ceases to be a fact of memory and becomes participation.
✦
X

The Four Types of Guilt

Jaspers does not provide simplified answers. He does something far harder — he splits the very concept of guilt, removing from it every comfort.

Criminal
Where there is an act, a crime, a concrete subject, a concrete accountability. The domain of courts and prosecution.
Political
Where a person belongs to a state acting in the name of many — even if they personally committed no crime. Belonging creates a form of responsibility.
Moral
Where the question concerns one's own conscience — inner compromise, silence, assent to a lie for the sake of comfort, fear or inertia.
Metaphysical
The hardest and most cutting level. It cannot be proved in court and is not distributed by role. It arises where a person can no longer honestly live as though what is happening in the world of people has nothing to do with them in a deep sense.

And here everything is cut to the quick. The language in which I still think and write this text, the memory in which my perception of truth and falsehood was formed, the entire inner geography of childhood — none of this has suddenly become foreign. It continues to live in me as damaged but still-functioning tissue.

✦
XI

Language as Inner Geography

I grew up in Latvia, in a Russian-speaking Soviet environment. I did not belong to Russia politically, but for a long time I belonged to it inwardly — through language, intonation, cultural air, through the very form of thought that seemed natural and required no defence against itself. Therefore the question of guilt for me never sounds like an external verdict. It sounds as a question about the moment at which the space one grew up inside ceases to be an environment and becomes a trial. Where cultural proximity ends and the necessity of an inner rupture begins.

✦
XII

The Linguistic Emigration

The language remains native, but the collectivity it once almost automatically pulled along can no longer be accepted as mine. I cannot discard this language, because it is written into the very fabric of my thought, into the rhythm of childhood, into memory, into the way speech arises in me. But I also cannot enter the collectivity that today too often demands of language not truth but complicity.

And therefore there arises perhaps the hardest form of emigration: not geographical, but linguistic. One in which a person cannot exit language as one exits a room, but no longer has the right to speak from a false "we." Only one thing remains: to bear witness in one's native language without a flag, without collective shelter, without the right to innocent belonging.
✦
XIII

On Purification Without Self-Deception

This is why the Jasperian question of purification matters to me. Not in a moralistic or ecclesiastical-rhetorical sense, but in a far stricter one. How is purification possible without self-deception. How does one pass through rupture without falling into either self-justification or a beautiful posture of repentance. How does one stop using the past as an alibi. How does one stop using complexity as a shield. How does one acknowledge the connection without substituting it with a false identity. How does one emerge from historical fog not with a new ideology but with a changed structure of consciousness.

And precisely here Humanitas in Jaspers begins to sound particularly strongly. Because purification for him does not reduce to moral bookkeeping. It is not simply the acknowledgement of the fact of guilt. It is the preservation of the human in oneself after guilt. Not to lose the person in the other, even when history pushes in the opposite direction. Not to convert clarity into coldness. Not to convert justice into a thirst for symbolic retribution.

✦
XIV

Freedom After Guilt

Here too arises one of the hardest questions in Jaspers. How is freedom possible after guilt. Not before it. Not before knowledge. Not before the fall. But after a person has learned about their connection to what is happening more than they would wish to know. After the old room has ceased to be home. Freedom here can no longer be simply the right to say: I am not with them. It must become the capacity to live further — without deceiving oneself either about the depth of the rupture, or about the fact that the former form of belonging has died.

XV

Rothko: the Second Axis

But even here thought only reaches the halfway point. Distinction clarifies, but does not hold. It strips away shelters but does not create a space in which one can stand after the shelters have been destroyed. And it is precisely here that Mark Rothko becomes necessary. Not as an artistic supplement to philosophy, but as a second axis. If Jaspers works through the question, the incision and the clarification, then Rothko does something different. He takes away. He removes everything that can become too quick a consolation.

After catastrophe, as if he is saying, we no longer have the right to the excessive image. To too many figures. To too much narrative. To too much cultural noise above the abyss. He removes the face, because after historical humiliation and mass death the face is no longer innocent. He removes the scene, because the scene too easily becomes the decoration of a lie. He removes the story, because the story too quickly returns to the person the illusion that everything can be placed in an intelligible sequence. And what remains is field. Colour. Boundary. Breath between layers. Almost nothing. But in this almost nothing there is discovered what was previously buried beneath cultural noise.

✦
XVI

Rothko as Emigrant — Proximity Without Distance

It is essential that Rothko was an emigrant. He lived far from the space of European catastrophe, but distance did not bring liberation. If your root reaches into Eastern European, Russian-imperial, Jewish space, if your early form of the world was shaped in the field of historical anxiety, exile and vulnerability — then the fire does not remain external, even if you are physically in New York. In this sense his emigration did not cancel the connection but made it more agonising, because the tragedy no longer dissolved into the environment but lived in him.

It is precisely this structure that feels close to me. Not because I went through the same thing. But because the question is the same: how does one live after the image has ceased to be truth. One can live in Dublin but not be outside. One can be at the other end of Europe and still have no right to clean distance.

After a certain catastrophe, the image ceases to be innocent. The face is no longer simply a face. The scene is no longer simply a scene. And then his refusal of figuration is not a modernist experiment, but because the old image can no longer bear truth. This is his gesture. Not flight into abstraction, but refusal of false consolation.
✦
XVII

The Room Assembles Itself

This is why his canvases are so tightly connected to the historical trauma of the Second World War, even when they illustrate nothing literally. He does not make a report on catastrophe. He creates a form of existence after catastrophe. Colour in his work does not decorate. It holds pressure. The boundary between colours does not draw objects but fixes the tension between darkness and the possibility of light, between memory and disappearance, between the inner cry and the impossibility of crying out. What in Jaspers is articulated as a boundary situation, in Rothko becomes visible as a condition: not an event that can be narrated, but a field in which the person is stripped of their familiar intermediaries.

Thus my room appears. But after the rupture it can no longer be an interior. Cannot be an archive of nostalgia. Cannot be a space where memory is comfortably arranged on shelves. If it is honest, it must become a chamber of clarification. A field from which nothing superfluous can now be removed.

✦
XVIII

The Structure: Arendt, Jaspers, Rothko

In this room Jaspers and Rothko meet naturally. One does not let me name what is happening simply as evil and thereby place myself outside. He returns the question to me, to my position, to my connection, to my responsibility, to how to distinguish. The other does not let me aestheticise this connection. He removes the narrative, the figure, the cultural softness and leaves presence in a field where there is no longer anything to defend oneself with.

And here Arendt becomes necessary again, because it is precisely she who shows how disintegration enters everyday life, how evil becomes possible through the destruction of the space between people, through thoughtlessness, through language that has lost its inner conscience.

And then the whole structure assembles itself. Arendt shows the mechanics of disintegration. Jaspers returns disintegration into the person as the question of guilt, boundary situation, purification, Humanitas and freedom. Rothko shows the condition after the collapse of the image, when only the field of presence remains. And I find myself between them — not as a centre, not as a hero, not as a judge, but as a point of tension at which all these questions become present tense.
✦
XIX

Why This Text Cannot Be Moralistic

This is why this text cannot be moralistic, or consolatory, or purely analytical. It is written as an attempt to no longer evade. Not to evade into nostalgia for the room that no longer exists. Not to evade into rage, which too quickly releases a person from inner complexity. Not to evade into false delicacy, which today is already becoming almost a form of moral fraud. And not to evade into simple condemnation, which allows one to feel right too early.

✦
XX

Russia as Ontological Threat to Latvia

Today Russia is for me not only a historical tragedy and not only a political problem. It has become an ontological threat to Latvia, to my native land. And here one can no longer hide behind cultural proximity, behind Russian language as an innocent environment, behind the Soviet past as a common room that can still be saved in memory without incision. But here too one cannot slide into the lazy denial of all things Russian as such. Both paths would be dishonest. The first would be blindness. The second would be laziness. I need a third path. The path of distinction. The path of clarification without self-deception.

Therefore my question is not only about what happened to Russia. It is also about what happened to me. In Russia there was a lengthy transformation — from Soviet space to a new form of will, in which resentment, the cult of force, the degradation of language and the normalisation of violence gradually composed a separate historical environment. But in me too a shift has occurred. I can no longer sustain the old form of inner equilibrium in which one could simultaneously be inside this cultural room and outside its political content. That form has collapsed. And therefore the question today is not whether I am thinking correctly. The question is what form of existence is still possible after this collapse.

✦
XXI

The Age of Fifty as a Hidden Boundary

Perhaps this is why the age of nearly fifty turns out to be here not a biographical detail but a hidden boundary. This is the age at which one can no longer live only by the accumulation of impressions, cultural refinement and deferred judgment. There comes a moment when certain things must become unbending. Not because one is definitively right. But because without this one ceases to hold oneself together.

I am not seeking reconciliation. And I am not seeking a definitive answer. The answer too often becomes a new form of shelter. What matters to me is different — to reach the point after which one can no longer speak as before. Can no longer use this language as innocent. Can no longer think of the past as a room one can still enter quietly. Can no longer pretend that the rupture happened only in history. It passed through me as well. After this, nothing becomes easier. But it becomes impossible to live as though nothing has happened.

Postscript — The Dagestan Dam and the Limit of Humanitas

A few days ago a dam burst in Dagestan. Heavy rains overfilled the reservoir, the water swept away houses, cars, entire streets. People died, including children. Thousands were left without shelter and everything they had built over decades. Under a post from a Dagestani charitable foundation collecting money for those affected, I saw comments. Many of them, from Ukrainians, sounded identical: "Allah punished," "this is for the war," "payback for the Kakhovka dam," "special water operation." With fire and laughing emojis.

I looked at these lines and thought: here it is, that very boundary about which I was writing above. But here it is important not to lie in the other direction. To understand is not to justify. And not to understand means to simplify into falsehood. Because behind these words stands not emptiness. Behind them stands experience. Destroyed cities. Murdered children. Blown dams. The very same Kakhovka, where the water also came not as a natural disaster but as a decision.

If this is not taken into account, any conversation about the human quickly becomes a cold abstraction, almost offensive to those who live inside this pain. But precisely here the next, harder incision arises. Because pain explains the reaction but does not make it truth. At a certain moment a substitution occurs — almost imperceptible. Suffering ceases to be only a wound and begins to become the basis for a new judgment about the world. Someone else's grief begins to be read as a sign: not as a tragedy but as retribution.

And then what Jaspers warned about happens. Metaphysical guilt ceases to be a question addressed to oneself and becomes an instrument of judgment over the other. "They deserved it" is no longer a cry of pain — it is a formula. A formula in which the person again ceases to be a person and becomes the bearer of someone else's guilt. Here passes the boundary that is almost impossible to hold. Because it is impossible to demand immediate purification from the victim, impossible to expect cold distinction from a person whose child has been killed. But if this distinction disappears completely, then what was being fought for disappears as well — not in a political sense but in a human one. Because then not the side changes, but the form of consciousness.

And it is precisely at this point that it becomes possible to see what Humanitas is — not as a word, but as a limit. Not as softness, not as forgiveness, not as the renunciation of anger, but as the capacity — even inside pain — not to finally cancel the distinction between the guilty and the innocent, between a person and a symbol, between justice and the desire for others to "hurt just as much." This is almost unachievable, sometimes impossible. But precisely here passes the line after which a person either remains within the human, or begins imperceptibly to cross into the same structure they are fighting against.

I do not remove political and moral responsibility from those Dagestanis who voluntarily went to war against Ukraine. It exists. But when water carries away a five-year-old girl, and someone places a laughing reaction under that — distinction disappears. And in that moment it is already impossible to say that this is simply a reaction. This is already a form.

In this room one cannot shelter in any of the simplified positions. Neither in "I am not with them," nor in "they all deserved it." One can only stand and hold the distinction where it tears first. Without shelter. Without justification. Without relief. After something like this, it is already impossible to speak as before.
