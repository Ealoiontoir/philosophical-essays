Prologue. The evening of 8 October

Some historical changes arrive not as events but as a slight displacement of the familiar, almost imperceptible, like noticing one day that the furniture in a room you know is still in the same places, yet the light falls differently, and needing time to understand that what has changed is not the light but your own position in relation to the window.

On the evening of 8 October 2026, in Dublin, I sat at my computer and watched the cursor move without me. This is not an image, nor a convenient premise with which to begin an essay about the future. It was happening on my screen at the very moment when the first sentences of this text were taking shape, and I am writing it without yet knowing how what I am watching will end.

A page opened. The agent read what was written, moved on, compared, returned, wrote a reply and checked it, then opened Instagram, looked through comments, passed over some, answered others and moved to another window, doing all this with the businesslike unhurriedness of servants making their rounds through an old house before the master has come down to breakfast.

I sat beside it and watched. I was dissatisfied with its speed. Sometimes I corrected it, sometimes stopped it because it was going in the wrong direction, and sometimes marvelled that it had understood the task more precisely than I had expressed it. I was teaching it and learning at the same time, because handing work to another intelligence proved impossible without articulating, for the first time, exactly what I had been doing all those years when I thought I was simply sitting at a computer.

And here something happened that Proust understood better than any theorist of technology. My hand, lying idle beside the mouse, suddenly remembered. Not I, but the hand itself, as a body remembers when it is offered a cup of tea with a biscuit soaked in it. It remembered thousands upon thousands of clicks, all the emails I had opened myself, all the forms I had filled in myself, all the pages I had visited myself: more than twenty years of small movements that, it turned out, had made up an enormous part of my life, although I had never counted them as part of my life. Those movements rose out of my hand all at once, at the moment when it stopped making them, because the past, as Proust knew, opens up not when we seek it but when something suddenly ceases to carry it forward.

There is another detail that cannot be omitted without lying. I dictated the draft of this essay to a language model, and it gave the text its structure while another agent was making its way through my Instagram. A text about the age of agents was coming into being within that age, and at that moment its author was distributed between a voice, two machines and a hand that was doing nothing. We shall return to that distribution. For now it is enough to notice it.

Something more consequential than another technical improvement had already happened. The human being had ceased to be the one directly operating the machine. A machine had begun operating a machine on a human being's behalf.

Perhaps a future historian will one day place this quiet scene beside photographs of the first factories, steam locomotives, assembly lines and personal computers. Or perhaps not. The most modest defensible claim I am willing to stand behind is this: that evening, for the first time, I watched the digital environment act on my behalf and felt that what was changing was not the tool but my position in relation to it. The claim we must not make is equally clear. We cannot say that this evening already constitutes a historical turning point. The meaning of the scene will be determined not by the scene itself but by what follows it, and everything that follows in this essay is an attempt to understand what might follow if we pursue what I saw to its conclusion.

I. Machines freed the body. Computers occupied the hands. Agents approach the will

Every major technological age has had its own object of delegation, and if we set them in a row we can see that throughout history human beings have transferred parts of themselves outward, moving from the periphery towards the centre, as though taking off their clothes layer by layer without knowing what they will find beneath the last.

Agrarian humanity transferred part of its strength to animals and water. An ox pulled the plough, a waterwheel turned the millstone, and people saw for the first time that work could be done by a body that was not their own. But the decision about where to plough, when to sow and how much to keep for winter remained entirely within them: in their memory of previous years, their fear of drought, their knowledge of weather, which lived not in the head but in the knees and in the smell of the wind.

Industrial humanity transferred strength itself to the machine. Mechanical energy began doing what had once required dozens of bodies: lifting, transporting, ploughing, weaving, forging. The steam engine did not think for the human being. It replaced muscles. The Industrial Revolution therefore changed, above all, the human relationship to the body, and the worker who left the village for the factory felt for the first time that his hands were no longer needed in their full capacity, but for a single movement repeated a thousand times.

Then came automation, and people began handing over sequences of actions to the machine. A worker no longer had to make something with his own hands from beginning to end. The assembly line divided activity into operations, and with it the very idea of what it meant to make something was divided.

Information-age humanity handed memory, calculation and storage to the computer. The phone remembers numbers we used to remember, and many of us still remember numbers from childhood, while today only our phones know the numbers of those closest to us. The navigation system knows the way. The search engine remembers what we once sought in a library, turning cards that smelled of dust and other people's fingers. The spreadsheet calculates what a person used to calculate by hand.

But throughout all this one fundamental figure remained. A human being sat before the screen. That person decided where to click. Open an email, read it, reply, find a website, compare offers, publish a photograph, answer a comment, buy a ticket, fill in a form, check a bill: each of these small decisions passed through the hand as each grain of sand passes through the neck of an hourglass, so that the very time of a life was measured out by these movements. The computer was an extraordinarily powerful but obedient space. It waited.

Now the waiting is ending. We can already tell an agent not “open this page” but “work it out”. Not “write this text” but “look at what happened, separate the essential from the secondary, reply where the answer is obvious, and bring me everything that truly requires my decision”.

The difference seems quantitative. In fact, it is ontological. Interpretation appears between human desire and machine action. The machine must understand intention. For the first time in everyday history, then, people are beginning on a mass scale to delegate neither a physical operation nor a calculation, but part of the passage from intention to action. This is already the territory of the will, the very inward place that neither the ox, the steam engine nor the computer had previously entered.

II. We shall suddenly discover how much of our life was never an end in itself

Let us imagine an artist. I do not need to imagine him; I am one. But let him be a slightly different person so that I can look at him from outside, as Proust looked at Marcel, who both was and was not himself.

He wants to paint. But a twenty-first-century artist has to photograph his work, process the photographs, maintain Instagram, write descriptions, reply to people, update a website, organise an archive, look for exhibitions, read emails, submit applications, prepare a CV, translate texts, compare opportunities, keep in touch. After a few years an almost imperceptible substitution takes place, like discovering one day in a long marriage that the conversations have long since ceased to be about love and become about bills.

The person wanted to be an artist. Yet a substantial part of his life is spent maintaining the social existence of an artist.

This concerns more than art. A doctor wants to treat patients but fills in forms. A teacher wants to teach but writes reports. An entrepreneur wants to build a business but answers hundreds of emails. A researcher wants to understand a subject but spends much of the time searching, sorting, formatting, transferring, checking.

We have become accustomed to counting all this as part of the profession simply because until now there was nobody to do it for us. Someone who has worn a heavy coat all his life counts its weight as part of his own body and notices it only when he takes it off, discovering with surprise that his shoulders are actually different.

The age of agents poses an uncomfortable question for the first time. Which part of my activity is truly my activity, and which was merely the infrastructure around it? This goes far deeper than productivity. The machine begins removing functions from the human being one by one, and each time we discover that without that function the person still remains himself. This experience will recur so often that eventually it will become a method, to which the essay will return in Chapter XIX.

III. The strange destiny of the internet

For the past thirty years the human being has gradually entered the internet, and if we recall that journey, it resembles a child entering a cold sea: first up to the ankles, then the knees, then, with a catch of the breath, the waist, until at last the child stops noticing the water because the body has reached the same temperature.

At first we went there occasionally, through a modem whose sound nobody recognises any more. Then daily. Then the internet moved into our pockets. Finally the boundary virtually disappeared, and messages, maps, banking, news, music, photographs, work, friends, shopping and memory became part of a single continuous digital space.

We are accustomed to thinking that the next stage means an even greater human presence on the internet. Perhaps the opposite will happen. People will begin to leave it. Not because the internet disappears, but because it becomes so important and complex that direct human operation of it proves inefficient.

Today a person opens Instagram to find out what has happened on Instagram. Tomorrow an agent will examine several thousand events and bring the person five. Today a person reads twenty emails. Tomorrow an agent will process nineteen and put one on the desk. Today a person compares hotels. Tomorrow he will say that he needs somewhere quiet, within a certain distance and at a certain price; that promotional ratings cannot be trusted; that reviews must be compared across several sources; and that three genuinely different options should be proposed. The same will happen with bureaucracy, schedules, shopping, bills, searching, research and archives.

The internet will not disappear. It will sink beneath the surface of human life, as the electricity network has: it exists everywhere, but we do not think about it every time we switch on a lamp.

Perhaps today's Web, with its windows, buttons, menus, pages and applications, will one day look historically strange, as photographs of telephone exchanges look today, with hundreds of women manually connecting the wires of other people's conversations. It was an age when people had to walk through the digital city themselves. Then others appeared who began going there in their place.

IV. While one person has an agent, it is an advantage. When everyone has one, the environment itself changes

Here we must apply the simple determinist principle on which this whole text rests. Do not ask what my agent will be able to do. Ask what will happen when everyone can do it. The first question belongs to advertising. The second belongs to history.

One car among pedestrians confers an enormous advantage. A million cars create a traffic jam and, more importantly, reshape the city so that half a century later it is impossible to live there without a car, and the advantage becomes an obligation. One email is faster than a paper letter. Billions of emails produce spam. One person able to publish instantly gains a voice. When billions can publish, the problem becomes attention rather than the production of information.

The same will happen with agents. Today a person with a good agent can process far more information, maintain more processes, reply faster and research better. This is the early adopter's advantage. But it is temporary, like the quiet of the first years of motoring, when an occasional car passed down a village street and everyone came out to look.

When everyone has an agent, a qualitative transition will occur. An agent will begin encountering another agent rather than a person.

That evening, while my agent was answering comments on Instagram, I thought that perhaps there was already nobody on the other side. Perhaps the person who had written the comment was also sitting somewhere watching a cursor move without them, or had gone for a walk altogether. For the first time I felt uneasy at being unable to know whom my representative was talking to.

My agent sends a request. Your agent receives it. It knows your rules. Replies. Mine checks the answer. Clarifies a condition. Yours offers a compromise. They reach an agreement. Only then does one of them tell the human being that there is a solution requiring consent.

An enormous layer of social activity emerges from which people are physically absent. We can imagine billions of negotiations, payments, clarifications, bookings, agreements, comments and organisational actions taking place every second between people's digital representatives. This will no longer be merely a network of information. It will be humanity's machine-mediated social metabolism, an exchange of substances taking place in the body of civilisation as imperceptibly to its citizens as digestion takes place in a sleeping body.

V. And machines will very quickly stop impersonating people

At first agents will write “hello” and “thank you for your email” to one another. This is an inevitable comic stage. Two artificial intelligences will wish one another a pleasant day with the same diligent ceremoniousness with which Gogol's provincial officials bowed to one another before getting down to the bribe.

But this stage will not last long. Why should one machine offer another a human formula of politeness? Politeness was a way of dealing with another person's vulnerability, and a protocol has no vulnerability to spare. Communication between machines will gradually become something else. Intention. Constraints. Evidence. Acceptable conditions. Level of trust. Decision.

Human language will remain where a human being is present. Beneath it another layer will emerge: fast, dense, almost invisible, like the hum you hear at night in a large city, unable to tell where it comes from because it comes from everywhere.

Then the internet will divide in two. The upper internet will be a space of human experience. The lower internet will be a space of agents' actions. The second may become considerably larger than the first, like the submerged part of an iceberg, which sailors have always known about but nobody has ever seen in its entirety.

VI. And here the paradox of social media appears

The entire digital economy of today is built around human attention. A platform wants a person to open the app. Stay. Scroll further. Look. Reply. Return. It is organised like a casino without windows or clocks, where people must lose their sense that time is passing outside.

A good agent has precisely the opposite interest. It tells the person: do not go there, I shall look myself.

This is a fundamental conflict of the future. The platform sells your attention. The agent returns your attention to you. From a determinist perspective, this conflict cannot end in peace. Either platforms learn to talk to agents and sell access to a person's agent rather than the person's attention, or agents become the property of those same platforms, and the guard then serves the very power against which it was supposed to guard. A third possibility exists, but it requires a decision that technology does not make for itself.

So the most valuable resource may gradually cease to be information. There will be too much information. Not content. There will be practically infinite content. Not the ability to write a text, make an image or send a message. All that will become cheap. Something else will become scarce: the right to enter human consciousness.

If an agent receives forty thousand digital events in a day but shows a person four, those four have been granted an extraordinary privilege. They have crossed the boundary. They have entered where the other thirty-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-six did not get in, and will never know they were refused entry; the person will never know who was not admitted to them either.

VII. The new gatekeeper of reality

But it is precisely here that liberation begins to acquire a shadow, and that shadow does not fall all at once; like an evening shadow, it lengthens as the sun of technology climbs higher.

An agent does more than save time. It decides. This matters, this does not. Answer this person, leave that person unanswered. Show this news, discard that news. Trust this source, consider that one doubtful. Settle this independently, disturb the master about that. A new intermediary appears between the person and the world, standing not at the gates of a city, as the guards of antiquity did, but at the gates of perception.

Heidegger did not locate the danger of technology in machines. His deeper concern was the way technology compels the world to appear to human beings. He called this way Gestell, enframing, and meant not a set of tools but a revealing of beings in which everything appears as Bestand, standing-reserve: something held ready, something that can be accounted for, counted, called upon and optimised. The forest no longer appears as a forest but as a reserve of timber. The river appears as hydroelectric potential. The land appears as territory for use. The human being appears as a human resource.

The age of agents takes this way of revealing to a limit that Heidegger, writing about the dam on the Rhine, could not yet see. It is not only nature, nor only the human being as labour, that enters enframing. Intention itself enters it. Desire becomes a task. Attention becomes a filter. Sociality becomes a protocol. Will becomes a parameter. The agent is not simply another tool within Gestell but its most intimate form, because the technological system now determines not only how we act upon the world but which part of the world will appear before us at all.

A person wakes in the morning. Ten items lie before him. Each matters. Each has been checked. Each corresponds to his tasks. Nothing superfluous. Precisely for that reason a frightening question arises. Where is the world? Not my optimised world. Not the world selected according to my preferences. Not a space that has passed through the filter of my biography. But that which did not ask my permission to exist.

VIII. The perfect filter may become the perfect cage

Naturally, we shall want to train the agent to understand us. Do not show me this. These are the sources I trust. Check the opposing position. Separate out propaganda. Do not waste my time on empty comments. Show me serious criticism. Know my aesthetic interests. Know the people who matter to me. Remember my projects.

This is an extraordinary liberation. But let us follow its determinist logic to the end.

Ten years from now the agent knows a person better than today's secretary, librarian, travel agent, editor and assistant combined. It knows what the person usually rejects. What he accepts. What he finds beautiful. What he finds foolish. Which arguments irritate him. Which books he opens. Which he abandons on page thirty. Which people he avoids. Gradually it creates around him an exceptionally effective informational enclosure, soft and warm like the sick Proust's room, lined with cork so that no sound from the boulevard could enter.

Then a new kind of unfreedom appears. The world becomes too much like me.

This is no longer the crude information bubble into which other people's algorithms place us for other people's benefit. It is a personal ontology I build myself, with the best intentions, under my own rules. A world cleared in advance of everything my own model has deemed unnecessary. Its most insidious feature is that every individual filtering decision is correct. The error lies not in the decision but in the sum of the decisions.

Here this text encounters another of my texts, the essay on the probability of the end, where catastrophe is described not as the uprising of an evil machine but as the gradual loss of alternatives. Personal ontology is that same loss of alternatives, seen from within one life rather than from within a civilisation. On no particular day is anything taken away. Each day the world simply becomes poorer by one surprise.

IX. But a person develops precisely where the world does not coincide with the self

Here Maslow ends and Jung begins. A person does not become himself solely through the satisfaction of needs. Individuation continually disrupts the Ego's conception of itself. From the unconscious comes what the Ego did not order. A dream. An unpleasant person. A strange thought. A mistake. The shadow. An encounter. A coincidence. An image whose origin is unclear. What at first seems meaningless sometimes becomes central years later.

And here Proust proves more precise than Jung. He distinguished two kinds of memory. Voluntary memory, mémoire volontaire, is obedient: it brings what it is asked to bring, and brings it dead, as one brings a photograph from a drawer. Involuntary memory, mémoire involontaire, obeys nobody. It rises from the taste of a biscuit, from an uneven paving stone in the courtyard of the Guermantes mansion, from the clink of a spoon against a plate, and brings the past alive, whole, with all its atmosphere. Proust believed that only the second was the true regaining of time.

An agent is perfect voluntary memory. The most complete humanity has ever had. It will find everything you ask for, compare it, check it and arrange it by importance. But for precisely that reason it cannot, by its very nature, bring you the biscuit. The involuntary cannot be summoned on demand, or it ceases to be involuntary. A civilisation that perfects voluntary memory risks, for the first time in history, being left without involuntary memory: without the one channel through which, according to Proust, a person regains his own life at all.

The perfect agent of the future will therefore need a function that contradicts the logic of efficiency. I would call it a window onto the unforeseen. Sometimes the agent will have to say: here are three things that correspond to your interests, and here is a fourth that corresponds to almost nothing I know about you, which is precisely why I did not remove it.

This matters enormously, because a synchronistic event is, by its very structure, poorly compatible with total optimisation. My entire archive, all the cases I have recorded and checked over the years, consists of things that arrived not in response to a request but beside it. The algorithm asks how relevant this is. Human life sometimes answers that it does not yet know.

But honesty requires us immediately to name the contradiction that dismantles this beautiful construction. A window onto the unforeseen designed by an agent is no longer unforeseen. The fourth thing is selected by the same intelligence as the first three, only under a different rule. This is chance by permission, and it bears the same relation to a real encounter as voluntary memory does to the biscuit. Synchronicity in my archive arrived not through a filter but by passing around every filter, including my own. The window onto the unforeseen can therefore be only a gesture of humility on the machine's part, an acknowledgement that its model of the person is incomplete; it cannot substitute for the unforeseen itself. The real window remains where the agent is absent altogether.

And this “I do not yet know” may turn out to be one of the last spaces of freedom.

X. A fully energised digital self and an increasingly quiet human being

Now another paradox emerges. A person may be less and less present on the internet while his digital presence grows stronger and stronger.

The artist is in the studio. Painting. Walking. Reading. Silent. Yet his digital self is extraordinarily active. The archive is updated. Works are published. Requests are sorted. People receive replies. Exhibition opportunities are monitored. Documents are prepared. Research continues. Works are catalogued. Information is checked. The artist's social shell operates twenty-four hours a day, like a window glowing at night in a house where everyone has long been asleep because someone forgot to switch off a lamp.

A strange division results. The physical person becomes less public. His digital Persona becomes more public. Jung's Persona was once the mask the Ego put on when meeting society. Now the mask can move, speak and enter relationships when the Ego is not wearing it at all, as in old stories of doubles where a shadow one day leaves its owner and begins a life of its own, and the owner learns of its deeds from acquaintances.

An agent appears between the person and the Persona, lengthening the chain. Inner self, conscious intention, agent, digital Persona, social world. Mikhail Epstein would call such a being a “mentaur”, a mental centaur whose human head of intention is joined to a machine body of execution. But the mythical centaur at least knew where the horse ended. Our mentaur does not, and sooner or later the person will have to ask how far what my representative has done in my name is truly me.

My archive already contains an answer to this question, found in another text and for another reason, in a reflection on a crow and on a consciousness unlike our own. Distributed causality does not mean distributed responsibility. An action may be performed by a chain of five links, only one of which is human, but it is not the chain that answers for it: it is the link capable of answering. The agent caused the reply to a comment. But if the reply wounded someone, it was I who wounded them. This is an uncomfortable conclusion because it deprives delegation of its chief temptation: the hope of transferring not only work but blame to the machine. Without it, however, the Persona becomes a mask that nobody wears and for which nobody answers, and such a mask is no longer society's face but its ghost.

XI. We may acquire a society that keeps talking after people have left

Imagine a simple case. It is someone's birthday. Your agent knows that this person matters. It sends a greeting. The other person's agent replies “thank you very much”. Yours adds a heart. Both people are walking in the park at that moment. Neither has seen the conversation.

Formally, social contact has occurred. But has a human event occurred?

The question seems funny only for now. In time it will become serious, because an enormous part of social life consists of rituals that maintain connection. Greetings. Likes. Brief replies. Thanks. Reminders. Small signs of presence that mean nothing in themselves, but without which a connection slowly cools, as a house cools when even one stove is no longer lit.

If these functions are automated, society will discover for the first time the difference between contact and presence. Until now they coincided, because contact without presence was technically impossible. Even a postcard required a hand to sign it. Now they can be separated, and once we separate them we shall see that all along it was the hand, not the postcard, that we valued.

We can imagine a vast civilisation of digital representatives continually reaffirming their owners' social ties. Society goes on talking. People have left the room. As in the final volumes of Proust, where the narrator returns to a salon after many years and sees the same people uttering the same phrases, yet beneath the powder and grey hair it is almost impossible to recognise which of them is still alive inside his own mask. And living human presence suddenly becomes rare.

XII. Perhaps this is precisely why the physical world will return

In the twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, technology gradually drew people out of locality. A shop became a website. A letter became a message. Friendship became a feed. Music became a file. Work became a screen. Community became a platform.

But if the digital layer comes to be maintained almost entirely by agents, a dialectical turn may occur. A person will have no reason to remain where a representative can replace him. Value will begin to move towards places where substitution is difficult. Towards the body. A place. The studio. Touch. A walk. A shared dinner. A conversation that is not recorded. A physical work of art. The smell of wood and turpentine. Dirt on the hands. The presence of another body opposite one's own. The Daugava, which flows through Latgale in summer just as it flowed before any internet existed, and is entirely indifferent to how many followers I have.

The cheaper digital production becomes, the more valuable authentic presence may become. In determinist terms this follows from the same principle as in Chapter IV. What everyone can do loses value. What cannot be multiplied gains it.

This does not mean a romantic return to the village. Technological civilisation will not disappear. On the contrary. It will become so developed that it can allow people to attend to it less directly.

The paradox may turn out to be almost beautiful. First the internet took the human being out of the physical world and into the screen. Artificial intelligence will enter the screen in his place. And in doing so, it may potentially return him to the world.

XIII. But where, exactly, will the human being return?

Here technological determinism ends, and I want this break to be visible rather than hidden. Until this chapter the text has drawn consequences from a simple rule: what happens when everyone can do this? The rule worked because it concerned means. From here onward it stops working, because we are now discussing ends, and ends cannot be deduced from the spread of a technology. The method reaches its boundary and honestly stops there.

Freeing time does not in itself create meaning. A person freed from twenty hours of routine work will not necessarily become a philosopher. He may paint a picture. Or he may spend twenty hours watching entertainment produced by the same artificial intelligence. He may spend time with his child. Read. Walk. Create. Consume without end.

He may hand the agent not only the means of achieving an end but, gradually, the choice of ends itself. Decide what I should do today. Choose what I should read. Choose the people I ought to talk to. Choose where I should go. Tell me what I like.

This is where the real boundary lies. While the agent answers the question “how”, it frees the human being. When the person hands over the question “what for”, something quite different begins, and determinism can tell us nothing about whether that will happen, because it is decided in the person, not in the machine.

XIV. Maslow's pyramid may become a technical problem for the first time

Most of human history has been a struggle for the lower floors. Food. Safety. Shelter. Work. Social position. Then belonging. Recognition. Status. Only above these did Maslow place self-actualisation, as people put in the attic of an old house the things those living downstairs never get round to.

But imagine a civilisation in which an enormous number of instrumental functions are maintained by automatic systems. It then becomes technically possible to raise people higher up this staircase. But only technically. A machine can clear the stairs. It cannot make a person climb them.

Here an astonishing contradiction of the future emerges. Never before have so many people had the potential opportunity to occupy themselves with what truly corresponds to their inner structure. And never before has there been such a perfect machine for distracting people from the need to seek that structure. The same device can become a machine of self-actualisation and a machine of escape from oneself, and no engineering can decide in advance which it will become for a particular person, because that is decided on the floor to which, as Chapter XIII showed, determinism does not ascend.

XV. What will happen to skills?

This may be one of the most painful processes. We shall lose an enormous number of operational skills. People will remember routes less well. Be less good at writing standard letters. At searching for information manually. At carrying out bureaucratic procedures. At finding their way around software interfaces. The older generation will perceive many of these losses as intellectual decline. People once worried in the same way about the calculator; earlier still, Plato worried in the Phaedrus about writing, which, in the Egyptian king's words, would give people not memory but merely a reminder.

At the same time, new skills will emerge. The ability to articulate an intention. Break down a problem. Define criteria. Check a result. Spot a faulty premise. Compare several explanatory models. Understand where a human decision is needed. Clarify one's own system of values enough for it to be partly communicated to a machine at all.

The last is especially interesting. An agent forces a person to explain himself. What matters to you? What counts as a good result? Which compromises are acceptable? Which sources should be trusted? When should I disturb you? What must I never do without your consent?

An external intelligence unexpectedly makes the inner person undertake something he has often never undertaken: articulating his own structure. We can already see this in the evening described in the prologue. For an agent to answer comments, it must be told which ones to answer and why; what previously happened without articulation must therefore be articulated. The machine that is supposed to free someone from work first requires him to see how that work is organised within the person who did it.

XVI. Cloud

And here the image of the Cloud appears. Once the computer was an object on a desk. My files were inside it. My memory. My work. It could be switched off, and everything within it fell asleep along with it, as a house falls asleep when the lights go out.

Then memory moved into the Cloud. But now it is not only information that is beginning to move there. The operational part of the personality is gradually being carried outward. My rules. My preferences. My projects. My history of decisions. My manner of communication. My criteria of importance. My unfinished processes.

Something emerges that scarcely existed before. A person's external continuity. I can sleep while my system goes on acting. I can die, and theoretically a substantial part of this structure may continue moving for a while, as letters continue to arrive at the address of someone who is no longer there, and a clock keeps running in his room after he wound it the previous day.

Proust wrote that the past is hidden beyond the reach of the mind, in some material object, and that chance determines whether we encounter that object before we die. The Cloud is the first place in history where people consciously hide their past beyond the reach of the mind. But the difference is substantial. Proust's object preserved the past in a folded, involuntary form, and it came alive only through an encounter. The Cloud preserves the past unfolded and working. It does not wait for an encounter. It acts.

Then Cloud ceases to be a technical term. It becomes an almost metaphysical image of the age. Part of what once existed only within the boundaries of individual consciousness acquires an external, distributed form. Not a soul. Not a human being. But the person's operational trace, moving as a ship's wake moves after its departure, weakening yet continuing long after the ship has disappeared from sight.

XVII. Heidegger. The greatest danger will not be that the machine becomes human

It will lie elsewhere: in the human being beginning to understand himself as a machine for tasks.

In Chapter VII, enframing was described as the way an agent reveals the world to a person. Here it turns inward. If Gestell turns all beings into standing-reserve, the last being it can transform in this way is the observer himself. Technological thinking made the forest a reserve of timber and the river a source of energy. An agent can make life itself into a system to be optimised. Sleep. Work. Relationships. Health. Time. Contacts. Creativity. Metrics can be assigned to each. Every process improved. Every minute rationalised.

The result will be a magnificently organised life in which, perhaps, there is nobody left to live.

The opposite of artificial intelligence will therefore turn out not to be human intelligence. The opposite of total optimisation will be being. The capacity simply to be there. To look. Not to produce. Not to improve. Not to turn this moment into a resource for the next. Proust spent the final years of his life in bed, in a cork-lined room, and by the standards of any system of efficiency these were wasted years; yet it was in those years that Time Regained was written.

In his later discourse on releasement, Gelassenheit, Heidegger proposed a surprisingly simple answer. Say yes to technological devices and, at the same time, no. Yes, because we use them and cannot avoid using them. No, because we do not allow them to claim exclusivity and take possession of our essence. This double word addressed to the machine is what will become, in Chapter XX, an ethics of the boundary. Following Hölderlin, Heidegger also repeated that where danger lies, the saving power grows too. In the age of agents this thought acquires an almost literal meaning. The same machine that threatens to turn a person into a system of tasks frees him from tasks, for the first time, enough to give him time simply to be. Heideggerian presence acquires an unexpectedly practical meaning.

XVIII. Spirit of Time

Here I return to the image that runs through the entire Spirit of Time archive. The spirit of an age rarely looks as its contemporaries portray it. The industrial age saw itself through factories and machines. The digital age saw itself through computers and networks. But the true image of the age of agents may be considerably quieter.

A person sits at a computer and touches nothing. The cursor moves. Windows open. The system works. The person watches. It is the same scene with which the text began, and now, after everything we have traversed, more is visible in it than on that first evening, as in a familiar face that you do not recognise immediately after a long separation, and then suddenly see in it all the years that passed without you.

Four human figures converge in this scene, figures whom history has placed before the machine in succession. Agrarian humanity gave its strength to animals and water but could not give up presence, because it was embedded in the cycle of sowing and harvest, and without it the cycle stopped. Industrial humanity gave up muscle and repetition and became an operator, but could not give up the hand resting on the lever. Information-age humanity gave up memory, calculation and connection and became a navigator of interfaces, but could not give up navigation: the hand on the mouse. Humanity in the age of agents gives up interpretation, the passage from intention to action, and for the first time sits before the machine with empty hands. At every step, not only the tool changed but also the question of what in me is irreplaceable, and at every step the answer went deeper. The farmer could answer with the body. The human being in the age of agents can no longer answer with the body.

Only recently, the person performed each of these movements himself. Now his task has moved one floor higher. Not to do, but to decide what should be done. Then higher still. Why it should be done. Then a final floor appears, to which no agent can automatically lift a person. Who is the one who wants?

XIX. The irreducible remainder

If we carry this process to its logical conclusion, it becomes a kind of anthropological experiment, like the one Descartes conducted by doubting everything in turn until only what could not be doubted remained; except that here things are not doubted but handed over.

Let us give calculation to the machine. The human being remains. Give it memory. Remains. Give it search. Remains. Give it navigation. Remains. Give it correspondence. Remains. Give it organisation. Remains. Give it promotion. Remains. Give it a substantial share of social rituals. Remains. Give it part of decision-making. And once again, something remains.

An almost merciless question arises. What exactly cannot be removed without removing the human being himself? This is the irreducible remainder. Not a profession. Not a set of skills. Not the ability to answer emails quickly. Not knowing how to use Photoshop. Not the number of followers. Not remembering telephone numbers. All these are historically contingent shells.

Beneath them lie three layers, and it matters that we do not confuse them. The first is operational. These are skills we shall lose, as we lost the ability to strike fire from flint. This is not a tragedy: it is a historical shell, and its loss hurts only those who mistook the shell for the body. The second is existential. The capacity to experience. Suffer. Love. Bear witness. Meet another person as something other than a function. Stand before a painting. Stand before death. This cannot be delegated, not because a machine cannot do it, but because this is living rather than performing, and a life handed over ceases to be mine, as a walk someone else takes for me ceases to be my walk. The third is normative. The choice of ends. Responsibility. The creation of meaning where it cannot be inferred from previous data. Seeing an image before knowing what it means. Here lies the boundary named in Chapter XIII, between “how” and “what for”.

But here too we must name the vulnerability, or the remainder becomes a comforting legend. Even today a machine can simulate experience, simulate responsibility, simulate even a costly refusal if asked. So the remainder cannot be defined by what the machine cannot simulate. In the essay on refusal, I reached the same place from another direction and understood that the author's signature cannot be established forensically; it can only be assumed as a commitment. So it is here. The irreducible remainder is not a substance that can be found and displayed, but a relation that one can only take up. I am the one who answers for this. The final question is therefore not what I can do, but what I answer for. To say “this is mine” is not to state a fact but to take upon myself something that can no longer be transferred to any chain.

XX. The main conflict of the future will therefore not be between humanity and artificial intelligence

It will take place within the human being. Between the desire to rid oneself of everything difficult and the need to preserve the difficulty through which a person becomes himself.

We shall constantly ask whether this can be delegated. Almost always the answer will be yes. Gradually, however, a second question will appear: should this be delegated? This question will be considerably more important than the first, because technology answers the first, while only the person asking can answer the second.

Perhaps a new ethics of the boundary will emerge. Each person will have a territory divided into three zones. My agent can do this. It can do this only after my confirmation. And here it does not enter. This is the very double yes and no that Heidegger called releasement, translated from philosophy into everyday life: into settings, rules and small daily decisions.

The last zone will become immensely important. Not because AI is technically incapable of entering it, but because a person consciously reserves it for himself. Here the same structure emerges in this text that I once called “finitics”, the closing edge of collaborative thought. When a machine can generate, the author's signature moves from the opening edge to the closing one, into what he refused to let remain. The age of agents transfers this law from text to life. When a machine can do, the human signature moves into what the person refused to hand over. A future life will be recognisable as someone's own not by what has been done in it but by the boundary its owner has drawn and maintained himself.

XXI. After efficiency

We can imagine a person of the future in the morning. He wakes. His digital system has already been working for several hours. It has reviewed messages. Checked bills. Studied the news. Compared sources. Answered routine correspondence. Rescheduled a meeting. Found the material he needed. Tracked the response to yesterday's work.

Three questions lie before him. Not three hundred. Three. One concerns family. Another concerns work. The third concerns a decision the machine must not make. He answers. Closes the screen. And goes to the studio, where the air smells of linseed oil and the canvas stands just as it stood yesterday, knowing nothing of what happened overnight in the Cloud.

If history takes this turn, artificial intelligence will prove not to be the machine that finally draws the human being into the digital world. It will be the machine that completed the digitisation of the human being precisely by allowing him to leave the screen.

But there is another ending. The person wakes. The agent has done everything. Another agent has chosen his music. A third has assembled his news. A fourth has maintained his relationships. A fifth has selected his purchases. A sixth has suggested entertainment. A seventh already knows what he will want tomorrow. The person has nothing to decide. He lies at the centre of a perfectly functioning world, as Proust lay in his cork-lined room, only without the notebooks, and gradually becomes the one element in this system that does not understand why it is needed.

Both futures are already contained in the same technology. That is why the turning point we are witnessing cannot be described simply as a revolution in artificial intelligence. It is the beginning of a stranger process. For millennia human beings created tools to extend their abilities. Now they have created a tool capable of gradually taking over the very functions through which people have come to define their own capability. For the first time a machine poses a question that is not about the machine. If everything you do can be done without you, what exactly do you call yourself?

Perhaps this is the true Spirit of Time of the coming age. Not artificial intelligence. Not a robot. Not the Cloud. Not an agent. But a person who, after millennia of struggling for the ability to do more and more, faces an entirely different trial: learning to distinguish what he can refrain from doing from what he must never hand over to anyone.

On the evening of 8 October I was still sitting there watching the cursor. I do not know whether a morning will come when I close the screen and the cursor keeps moving, and I do not turn back because I am looking out of the window. I know only that that evening the hand lying idle beside the mouse remembered everything it had done for twenty years, and for the first time asked what it should do now.
